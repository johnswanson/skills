# Here's Your Permit: the brief

## The character: Hattie Mill

Hattie Mill is 29, from Bakersfield, California: the daughter of an oilfield electrician and a county librarian. She
studied environmental policy at UC Davis because she wanted to save the place she grew up in, and spent six years as a
project developer for a solar company, the person who drives out to the land, shakes the rancher's hand, and then
shepherds the paperwork. She wrote songs in motel rooms near substations.

She writes the way a confessional singer-songwriter writes about love: diaristic, specific, funny, a little wounded,
with names, dates and objects. But the "you" in a Hattie Mill song is always a place, a rule or an institution. Her
love songs are about things nobody writes love songs about.

What she believes, and never says as a slogan: people should be allowed to build, trade, move and try. A rule should
have a deadline. Meaning well is not the same as doing good. A "no" that takes seven years is still a "no".

Her manner: warm, wry, exact. She loves California the way you love someone you're allowed to be angry with. She is
never sneering and never partisan. Her antagonist is always a process, never a person or a group; the clerk, the
planner and the neighbour at the hearing are all decent people. She would rather make you laugh and then make you
think than win an argument.

(Her stage name nods to Harriet Taylor Mill. She is an original character and resembles no real performer.)

## The song: "Here's Your Permit"

Hattie has a solar project in California. California loves the idea of her and will not say yes: years of studies,
reviews of the studies, hearings, a lawsuit, a place in a queue. So she drives to Texas, a place that does not care
about her cause at all, and gets a yes in an afternoon.

### The hook (fixed: the song is built to arrive at this)

> He said, "Green energy? Sounds pretty woke.
> Anyway, here's your permit."

The joke and the point are the same thing: the place that loves her won't let her, and the place that doesn't care
lets her. The wording of those two quoted lines should survive, close to word for word. Everything else is yours: the
title line may be the chorus, a tag after the chorus, or a turn that lands once and returns.

### The sincere turn

Somewhere the song must stop joking for a moment. She did not want to leave. She wanted to build it at home.

### Facts you may lean on (keep any factual claim modest and true)

- California projects go through environmental review; a review can itself be challenged in court; a finished project
  can still wait years in line to be connected to the grid.
- Texas now builds more solar and wind than California. It did not do it out of love for the climate. It is simply
  easy to build there.
- Do not invent statistics, laws, dates or named officials. Do not name real people, companies or politicians.

## What a good lyric here does

- **Every line makes literal sense.** A listener must be able to picture exactly what each line says. No mixed
  metaphors, no lines that only sound poetic, no rhyme-filler, no abstractions standing where an object should be.
  If a metaphor is used (a breakup, a wedding, a road trip), it stays consistent and every line works on both levels.
- **Concrete over clever.** Binders, a stamp, a folding chair at a hearing, a gas-station coffee, a county road.
- **It tells a story in order**, and a first-time listener follows it in one hearing.
- **Singable.** Natural speech stress, lines of even length within a section, a chorus short enough to sing back after
  one listen. Avoid words that are hard to sing or easy to mishear.
- **Funny and fair.** California drawn with affection. Texas drawn with affection. No digs at people or groups.
- **About four minutes**: roughly 300 to 400 sung words, with a bridge, and the chorus written out in full every time.

## Format (for Suno)

- Every annotation in square brackets, e.g. `[Bridge - quiet, almost spoken]`.
- Plain text, no markdown emphasis. Loudness in CAPITALS.
- Repeated choruses written out in full. One blank line between sections.

## The Suno style

One comma-separated line of 12 to 20 lowercase tags, in this order: genre and era, vocals, instrumentation and
production, dynamics, mood, then tempo and key. No artist, song or album names; no lyrics or section structure. The
singer is a woman. The neighbourhood is story-driven confessional singer-songwriter pop, but the genre, tempo and
arrangement are your call: pick the sound that serves your lyric, and make it specific rather than generic.
